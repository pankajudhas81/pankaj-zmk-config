## 1. What Are Keycaps?

A keycap is the plastic cap that sits on top of a keyboard switch. It is the part your fingers actually touch — the only part of the keyboard that the user physically contacts hundreds of thousands of times per year.

Here is the crucial insight that surprises most newcomers: **keycaps have almost nothing to do with switch feel**. The tactile bump of a Gateron Brown, the linear smoothness of a Holy Panda, the satisfying click of a Cherry MX Blue — all of that is entirely the switch. The keycap sits on top and rides along for the journey.

What keycaps *do* affect:

| Dimension | How keycaps affect it |
|---|---|
| **Sound** | Material, wall thickness, and profile all dramatically change the thock, clack, or tock of a board |
| **Aesthetics** | The single biggest visual variable in any build |
| **Finger comfort** | Sculpt, texture, and surface shape under the fingertip |
| **Typing feel** | Texture (smooth ABS vs. matte PBT), top surface shape (flat vs. spherical dish) |
| **RGB** | Whether light shines through and how |
| **Legend durability** | How long before the letters fade |

This is why keycaps are treated as an entirely separate purchasing decision from switches and boards. You can and should mix, match, and upgrade them independently. A $30 keyboard can be transformed with premium keycaps. A $400 keyboard can be ruined by garbage stock caps.

## 2. Anatomy of a Keycap

Every keycap has the same basic structure, though the proportions vary enormously between profiles.

### Cross-Section Diagram

```
  ┌───────────────────────────────────┐
  │   T O P   S U R F A C E          │  ← Legend lives here (the letter/symbol)
  │         (legend area)             │

           │              │             ← Side walls (thickness matters for sound)
           │  Interior    │
           │  (hollow)    │
           │              │
           └──────┬───────┘
                  │
            ┌─────┴─────┐               ← Stem socket (grips the switch stem)
            │   S T E M │
            │  S O C K ET│

            │  │  +  │  │               ← MX cross stem (the + shape that grips Cherry-style switches)
            │  └─────┘  │
            └───────────┘
                  │
          ════════╪════════             ← Switch stem (this is part of the SWITCH, not the keycap)
                  │
```

### Component Breakdown

**Top Surface**
The face of the keycap. Can be flat, slightly curved, or deeply dished (spherical). The shape determines where your finger naturally rests and how much "homing" the keycap provides. Some keycaps have a sharp edge around the legend area; premium keycaps have smooth, sculpted tops.

**Legends**
The printed, engraved, or molded text/symbols on the keycap. Can appear on the top (standard), front face (for special reference or style), or sides. The method by which legends are applied is critical to durability — more on this in [Section 5](#5-legend-methods).

**Side Walls**
The vertical sides of the keycap. Thickness here (measured in millimeters) affects acoustics significantly: thicker walls = deeper, lower-pitched sound. Thin walls can produce hollow clacking. Premium keycaps like GMK Cherry profile are known for their thick side walls.

**Stem Socket**
The hollow receptacle on the underside that grips the switch stem. This is where compatibility lives — the socket shape must match the switch stem shape. The three main stem types:

- **MX Cross (+)**: The universal standard. A plus-shaped post developed by Cherry and now used by Gateron, Kailh, Durock, NovelKeys, JWK, and virtually every switch manufacturer. When people say "MX-compatible," this is what they mean.
- **Alps/Matias**: A rectangular post with a different footprint. Found on vintage Alps keyboards (used in many beloved 80s/90s boards) and modern Matias switches. Completely incompatible with MX keycaps.
- **Topre**: A proprietary capacitive switch design. Keycaps for Topre have a different socket and are specific to HHKB and Realforce boards. Some Topre boards use adapters to accept MX keycaps, but native Topre keycaps don't fit MX switches.

**Wall Thickness**
Measured from the inside hollow to the outer wall. ABS stock keycaps are often 1.2–1.4mm thick. Premium PBT double-shot sets like those from Ducky, Varmilo, or GMK run 1.5–2.0mm. Thicker = more material = more mass = deeper, less hollow sound when a key is struck. This is the hidden acoustic variable that most people don't realize matters.

## 3. Keycap Materials

The material a keycap is made from determines its texture, durability, appearance, sound, and how it ages over years of use. There are four main materials you'll encounter.

---

### ABS — Acrylonitrile Butadiene Styrene

The most common keycap plastic in existence. Almost every stock keycap that ships with a gaming keyboard or mainstream board is ABS.

**Characteristics:**
- **Surface feel**: Smooth, slightly slick right out of the box. This smoothness intensifies over time.
- **Aging**: ABS develops a well-known problem called "shine" — the oils from your fingertips gradually polish the surface, creating a greasy, glossy appearance on the most-used keys (especially A, S, D, E, space). This process takes months to years depending on usage. Many people love the worn-in look; many hate it. Either way, it's inevitable.
- **Colors**: ABS accepts dye very readily, producing vivid, saturated colors. This is why the most visually striking keycap sets (think GMK sets with their incredible colorways) are ABS.
- **Manufacturing**: Easier and cheaper to mold than PBT. This enables the double-shot process (see [Section 5](#5-legend-methods)) at commercially viable prices.
- **Sound**: Generally produces a higher-pitched clack. Thinner ABS can sound hollow.
- **Thickness**: Typically thinner, though premium ABS (GMK sets) are notably thick.

**The GMK standard**: GMK (a German manufacturer) makes what many consider the finest ABS keycaps in the world. Their Cherry-profile double-shot ABS sets are the benchmark of the hobby — thick walls, precise tolerances, and unmatched colorway design. A GMK set costs $150–200+ and may involve waiting 6–18 months in a group buy. Worth it for the right person.

**When to choose ABS:**
- You want the most vibrant, accurate colors
- You're buying a premium double-shot set (GMK, SP, etc.)
- You prioritize looks and don't mind developing shine over time
- You want the widest selection of keycap sets

---

### PBT — Polybutylene Terephthalate

The enthusiast's default choice. PBT has become the dominant material for anyone who builds custom keyboards seriously.

**Characteristics:**
- **Surface feel**: Textured, slightly granular, matte. Noticeably different from ABS — more grippy under the finger, less slick.
- **Aging**: Resists shine far better than ABS. PBT keycaps used for years still look matte and relatively new. The oils don't polish PBT the same way.
- **Colors**: More limited than ABS due to how PBT accepts dye. Deep black, white, gray, pastels — all work beautifully. Highly saturated neons and certain bright colors are harder to achieve. This is why many PBT sets have a more restrained, sophisticated palette.
- **Manufacturing**: Harder to mold than ABS. PBT shrinks during cooling, making it harder to produce consistent tight tolerances. This is why PBT double-shots are harder to find and why dye-sublimation is more common on PBT.
- **Sound**: Slightly higher pitched than thick ABS. Many users find PBT to have a pleasant "thock" at medium thickness, especially on foam-dampened builds.
- **Thickness**: Generally thicker than ABS stock keycaps. 1.5mm walls are common in budget PBT; 2mm+ exists in premium PBT.

**When to choose PBT:**
- You type a lot and want keycaps that age gracefully
- You prefer a textured, matte feel over slick smoothness
- You care more about durability than color vibrancy
- You're on a budget (PBT blank or dye-sub sets can be very affordable)
- You're building an ergo/split keyboard and want keycaps that will last

**Popular PBT options:**
- KBDfans PBT dye-sub sets (budget-friendly, excellent)
- Enjoypbt sets
- Ducky/Varmilo stock PBT (bundled with keyboards, surprisingly good)
- Drop + Biip Extended 2048 (a famous PBT dye-sub set)

---

### POM — Polyoxymethylene (Delrin)

A specialty engineering plastic that has recently gained traction in the keyboard hobby.

**Characteristics:**
- **Surface feel**: Extremely smooth, almost self-lubricating. POM has naturally low friction properties.
- **Sound**: Produces a uniquely dampened, deep, muted thock. POM keycaps are often sought specifically for their acoustic properties.
- **Color**: Very limited — usually only available in black or white/natural.
- **Legends**: Because of POM's density and composition, legends are difficult to apply. Most POM keycaps are sold blank or with laser-etched legends.
- **Durability**: Very good. Doesn't shine like ABS.

**When to choose POM:**
- You want the deepest, most muted sound signature
- You prefer blank keycaps (no legends)
- You're building a "stealth" aesthetic build
- You love a smooth, almost buttery surface feel

---

### PC — Polycarbonate

Polycarbonate is transparent or translucent, making it the material of choice for RGB shine-through applications.

**Characteristics:**
- **Appearance**: Clear, frosted, or lightly tinted. When paired with RGB switches, light shines directly through the keycap body.
- **Feel**: Smooth, similar to ABS.
- **Use case**: Most commonly used for the stem area in double-shot keycaps where you want light to come through, or for fully transparent "shine-through" keycap sets.
- **Durability**: Good, but can yellow slightly over years of UV exposure.

**When to choose PC:**
- RGB is central to your build and you want maximum light diffusion
- You want a "ghost" or transparent aesthetic
- You're building an underglow-focused keyboard

---

### Material Comparison Table

| Material | Texture | Shine Resistance | Color Range | Durability | Price | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ABS | Smooth | Poor (shines with use) | Excellent | Good | $ to $$$$ | Vibrant colorways, premium sets |
| PBT | Matte/textured | Excellent | Limited | Excellent | $ to $$$ | Long-term use, ergo boards |
| POM | Very smooth | Excellent | Very limited | Excellent | $$ | Muted acoustics, blank builds |
| PC | Smooth | Good | Limited (clear/tinted) | Good | $$ | RGB shine-through |

## 4. Keycap Profiles (Shape)

This is, without question, the most important section for anyone building a custom keyboard. The profile of a keycap determines its height, the angle of its top surface, and whether the shape varies row by row.

Getting this wrong means an uncomfortable keyboard. Getting it right can transform typing into something that feels almost effortless.

---

### What "Profile" Means

Every keycap has two key shape properties:

**Height**: How tall the keycap is from the plate to the top of the cap. Measured in millimeters. Ranges from about 3.5mm (very low, like G20) to 16mm+ (very tall, like SA).

**Sculpted vs. Uniform**:
- **Sculpted (row-dependent)**: The shape, angle, and sometimes height of the keycap changes depending on which row of the keyboard it sits on. Row 1 (number row) may tilt back. Row 3 (home row) is usually the reference. Row 4 (bottom row) tilts forward. This creates an ergonomic wave that matches the natural angle your fingers approach keys from different distances.
- **Uniform**: Every keycap is identical regardless of row. You can put any key in any position and it feels the same. This is enormously important for programmable keyboards like the Corne where you may frequently remap keys or have non-standard layouts.

**Important for Corne/Split users**: Because the Corne uses a fully custom Miryoku-style layout where every layer rearranges what keys do, **uniform profiles are strongly recommended**. If you use a sculpted profile like Cherry or OEM, moving a key from one row to another means the angle feels wrong for that key's new position. With DSA or XDA (both uniform), every key sits the same — layout freedom without ergonomic compromise.

---

### OEM Profile

The profile that ships on the vast majority of gaming keyboards and mainstream boards. If you've ever used a standard retail keyboard, you've used OEM.

- **Height**: Tall (similar to Cherry but slightly taller)
- **Sculpted**: Yes, across 4 rows
- **Top surface**: Slightly angled, mild cylindrical curve
- **Feel**: The "default" feel most people grew up with
- **Sound**: Medium-high pitched on standard boards
- **Availability**: Extremely common, cheap, included with almost every retail keyboard
- **Best for**: People who want the familiar feel, budget builds
- **Drawbacks**: Not as refined as Cherry profile; tall height means more finger travel

Row breakdown:
- R1 (function row / number row): Tilted backward steeply
- R2 (top alpha row: QWERTY): Moderate backward tilt
- R3 (home row: ASDF): Nearly flat, slight tilt — the reference row
- R4 (bottom row: ZXCV): Tilted forward

---

### Cherry Profile

The enthusiast community's most popular sculpted profile. Developed by Cherry (the switch maker) for their keyboards, now produced by GMK and many others.

- **Height**: Medium — shorter than OEM by 1–2mm
- **Sculpted**: Yes, across 4–5 rows (R1-R5 with slight variations)
- **Top surface**: Cylindrical (curves in one axis, flat in the other)
- **Feel**: Shorter travel to each key, feels more controlled and precise than OEM
- **Sound**: Very good — thick GMK Cherry caps have legendary acoustics
- **Availability**: GMK sets (ABS), many dye-sub PBT sets use Cherry row sculpt
- **Best for**: Long typing sessions on standard layouts, anyone who finds OEM too tall
- **Drawbacks**: Premium sets are expensive; not ideal for 40%/ergo (sculpted, so row matters)

Cherry profile is the gold standard for enthusiast builds on standard layouts. If you have a TKL, 65%, or 60% with a mostly standard layout, Cherry profile is the answer most experienced builders will give you.

---

### SA Profile

SA (Spherical All) was originally designed by Signature Plastics and evokes the tall, chunky keycaps of classic computing from the 1970s and 1980s. Think IBM terminals, vintage DEC keyboards, PDP-11 workstations.

- **Height**: Very tall — among the tallest modern profiles (~16mm on the home row)
- **Sculpted**: Yes, with 7 distinct row profiles (R1-R7)
- **Top surface**: Spherical dish — curves in both axes, creating a concave bowl
- **Feel**: You sink into the key. The spherical top "catches" the fingertip. Retro, unique, polarizing.
- **Sound**: Loud. Very loud. The tall walls, hollow interior, and ABS material (most SA is ABS) produce a sharp, high-pitched CLACK that carries across a room.
- **Availability**: Mostly through group buys (Signature Plastics / SP, various designers). Some in-stock options.
- **Best for**: Enthusiasts who love the vintage aesthetic, display boards, people who loved their IBM terminals
- **Drawbacks**: Very tall — requires significant wrist angle adjustment. Loud. Heavy. Not suitable for all desk setups.

---

### DSA Profile ⭐ Best for Corne

DSA is likely the most practical profile for anyone using a 40%, split, or programmable ergo keyboard. It was developed by Signature Plastics as a low-profile uniform alternative to SA.

- **Height**: Short to medium (~7.6mm)
- **Sculpted**: **No — fully uniform**. Every key is identical.
- **Top surface**: Spherical dish (like SA, but shorter and gentler)
- **Feel**: Low, comfortable, the spherical top provides mild fingertip guidance without being aggressive
- **Sound**: Medium pitched, fairly quiet due to shorter walls
- **Availability**: Many options — SP makes DSA; many manufacturers make DSA-compatible sets
- **Best for**: **Ergo keyboards, split keyboards, 40%s, any programmable board with non-standard layout**
- **Why it's perfect for Corne**: Your Miryoku layout has keys in positions that don't correspond to their "traditional" rows. With DSA, a key moved from row 1 to row 3 feels identical. No weird angles.

Notable DSA sets:
- **DSA Milkshake** — pastel colors, beloved by the ergo community, works perfectly on Corne
- **DSA Hana** — Japanese-inspired, subtle and beautiful
- **SP DSA blanks** — Signature Plastics sells blank DSA in many individual colors for complete customization
- **EnjoyPBT blanks in DSA-compatible profiles** — budget option

---

### XDA Profile ⭐ Also great for Corne

XDA is a Chinese-originated profile that has become extremely popular in the enthusiast community for uniform ergo builds. It sits slightly taller than DSA with a flatter top.

- **Height**: Medium (~9mm)
- **Sculpted**: **No — fully uniform**
- **Top surface**: Flat (not spherical like DSA). Wider flat area than most profiles.
- **Feel**: Slightly more square-edged than DSA. Some prefer the flat top; some prefer DSA's dish.
- **Sound**: Medium
- **Availability**: Excellent — many budget Chinese manufacturers produce XDA, including KBDfans
- **Best for**: Ergo/split keyboards, anyone who prefers flat tops, budget builds
- **Drawbacks**: Wide flat top means less fingertip centering guidance

Notable XDA sets:
- **XDA Oblique** — a beloved set specifically popular with ergo builders
- **XDA Canvas** — clean, versatile
- KBDfans XDA sets are widely available and affordable

---

### MT3 Profile

MT3 was designed by Matt3o (a legendary figure in the keyboard community) and manufactured by Drop. It aims to recreate the feel of vintage computing keyboards, particularly the IBM Model M and Beam Spring boards that many typists consider the pinnacle of the craft.

- **Height**: Tall (similar to SA range)
- **Sculpted**: Yes, across 5 rows
- **Top surface**: Deep spherical dish — significantly more concave than SA
- **Feel**: Your finger sinks into the key meaningfully. The deep dish provides exceptional centering and a unique tactile sensation.
- **Sound**: Deep, full thock — the combination of height and deep dish creates excellent acoustics
- **Availability**: Primarily through Drop (drop.com); several sets produced in the MT3 profile
- **Best for**: Typists who want a premium, unique feel; anyone who loved vintage IBM keyboards; people who prioritize typing feel above all
- **Drawbacks**: Very tall, requires wrist support; only available from Drop ecosystem

Notable MT3 sets:
- **MT3 /dev/tty** — the original, classic green-on-dark-gray hacker aesthetic
- **MT3 Camillo** — Italian-inspired, beautiful colors
- **MT3 Serika** — clean, white-dominant
- **MT3 Dasher** — vintage terminal homage

---

### KAT Profile

KAT (Keyreative All-Touch) is a medium-height uniform profile produced by Keyreative.

- **Height**: Medium (~10mm)
- **Sculpted**: **No — uniform**
- **Top surface**: Slight spherical curve
- **Feel**: Somewhere between XDA (flat) and DSA (spherical dish)
- **Best for**: Ergo boards, people who want uniform with slightly more height than DSA/XDA
- **Availability**: Primarily through group buys

---

### G20 Profile

G20 is the flattest keycap profile commonly available. Almost no height above the switch top.

- **Height**: Very low (~3.5mm above plate)
- **Sculpted**: No — uniform
- **Top surface**: Flat with rounded edges
- **Feel**: Unusual — almost like having no keycaps at all. Very close to the board surface.
- **Best for**: Niche use — people who want the absolute lowest possible height; some laptop replacement builds
- **Availability**: Limited — Signature Plastics makes G20; not widely stocked

---

### MDA / KDA and Other Medium Uniform Profiles

Several other profiles occupy the medium-height uniform space:
- **MDA**: Medium height, uniform, slight spherical dish. Made by various manufacturers.
- **KDA**: Similar to MDA, slight variations in top surface curve.
- These profiles are less common and occupy the space between DSA and XDA without strong differentiation. Buy them if you find a set you love the aesthetics of.

---

## 5. Legend Methods

"Legends" are the printed characters, symbols, and labels on keycaps. How those legends are created determines how long they'll last, how they look, and what design possibilities exist.

---

### Double-Shot Molding ⭐ Best durability

The premium method. Two separate plastic components are molded together to create a single keycap.

**How it works:**
1. The inner piece (the legend shape) is molded first in one color
2. The outer shell (the keycap body) is molded around it in a second color
3. The two pieces fuse into a single inseparable unit

The legend is literally a different piece of plastic embedded inside the keycap. Since it's not on the surface — it IS the surface at that point — it cannot wear off. Ever. Double-shot legends are essentially permanent.

**Advantages:**
- Legends that will never fade, no matter how much you type
- Clean, sharp edges on legends
- Can achieve crisp legends in any color
- Premium tactile feel if the inner material is slightly different

**Limitations:**
- More expensive to manufacture (two injection molds per color combination)
- Color combinations have constraints: the legend color is determined by the inner plastic, so you need a separate mold run for different legend colors
- Not all color combinations are practical: getting a light legend on a dark keycap is easy; getting a photorealistic gradient is impossible

**Who uses it:** GMK, SP, Novelkeys, many enthusiast keycap manufacturers.

**Double-shot with PC top layer**: A variant where the top layer is transparent polycarbonate and the inner legend piece is a solid color. Allows RGB to shine through the transparent areas around the legend while the legend itself remains readable. This is how many "shine-through" keycap sets work.

---

### Dye-Sublimation

Heat forces dye molecules directly into the plastic material, creating legends that are part of the plastic itself — not on the surface.

**How it works:**
1. Dye is transferred to a transfer paper via printing
2. The keycap and paper are exposed to high heat and pressure
3. The dye sublimates (converts to gas) and penetrates the plastic
4. The dye re-solidifies inside the plastic material itself

The result: legends that cannot be worn off because they're inside the material.

**Advantages:**
- Extremely durable — comparable to double-shot in longevity
- Can achieve highly detailed artwork, gradients, and complex designs
- Works beautifully on PBT (PBT's porous structure accepts dye readily)
- Often more affordable than double-shot

**Limitations:**
- **Critical limitation**: Dye-sub only works when the dye is darker than the plastic. You cannot dye-sublimate white legends onto a black keycap — the dye can only darken the plastic, not lighten it. This is why many dye-sub PBT sets have dark legends on light keycaps, or why reverse dye-sub (dye the entire keycap except the legend area) is used for light-on-dark.
- Requires precise heat control; over-heating distorts PBT
- Reverse dye-sub (for light legends) is more expensive

**Best material pairing:** PBT. ABS can do dye-sub but PBT is the standard.

**Notable dye-sub sets:** Drop + Biip Extended 2048, EnjoyPBT Blank, many KBDfans sets.

---

### Laser Etching / Laser Engraving

A laser burns or removes material from the keycap surface to create the legend.

**How it works:**
The laser ablates (removes) a thin layer of the keycap surface, creating a visible engraved mark. Some methods then fill the etched channel with colored material.

**Advantages:**
- Cheap and fast
- Can produce fine detail
- Works on any material

**Limitations:**
- Legends fade over time as the etched surface wears
- Surface feel can be rough at the legend
- On dark keycaps, the ablated area is lighter; on light keycaps, darker — limited color options
- Not durable enough for heavy daily use over years

**Common where you'll find it:** Cheap gaming keyboards, some backlit keyboards (laser etching allows backlighting to show through), budget options.

---

### Pad Printing

Ink is physically applied to the keycap surface using a silicone pad, similar to a rubber stamp.

**How it works:**
A cliché (etched plate) holds ink, which is picked up by a silicone pad and deposited onto the keycap surface.

**Advantages:**
- Extremely cheap
- Can do any color combination

**Limitations:**
- The worst durability of any method. The ink sits on the surface and wears off with normal use. On heavily used keys, legends can disappear within months.
- Visual quality often poor — legends can look blurry or misaligned

**Where you'll find it:** Cheap retail keyboards, dollar-store level products. Avoid if possible.

---

### UV Printing

A modern inkjet-based printing method using UV-cured inks.

**How it works:**
Inkjet printer heads deposit UV-sensitive ink onto the keycap surface. UV light immediately cures (hardens) the ink.

**Advantages:**
- Full-color capability — can print photorealistic images, gradients, complex art
- Can do any color combination without two-shot molding
- Relatively affordable for small runs

**Limitations:**
- Durability better than pad printing but still surface-level — will eventually wear on heavy-use keys
- May develop cracking over time as the ink layer flexes with temperature changes
- Not yet at double-shot or dye-sub durability levels

**Where you'll find it:** Novelty sets, artisan-style production keycaps, some gaming-focused sets with complex art.

---

### Legend Method Comparison

| Method | Durability | Color Options | Cost | Found On |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Double-shot** | Permanent | Good (not gradients) | $$$$ | Premium enthusiast sets |
| **Dye-sublimation** | Excellent | Limited (dark on light) | $$-$$$ | PBT enthusiast sets |
| **UV printing** | Good | Excellent | $$ | Gaming sets, novelty |
| **Laser etching** | Fair | Limited | $ | Budget gaming keyboards |
| **Pad printing** | Poor | Any | $ | Cheap retail keyboards |

## 6. Compatibility

Before buying any keycap set, you need to confirm compatibility. Getting this wrong means keycaps that don't fit your switches.

---

### MX-Compatible — The Universal Standard

Cherry MX switches introduced the cross-shaped (+) stem design, which has become the de facto standard for mechanical keyboards worldwide. Every keycap marketed as "MX-compatible" uses the same stem socket dimensions.

**Switches that are MX-compatible (non-exhaustive):**
- Cherry MX (all variants)
- Gateron (all variants — Red, Yellow, Brown, Blue, Green, Milky, etc.)
- Kailh (most variants — Box switches, Speed switches, standard switches)
- Durock
- JWK / Everglide
- NovelKeys
- Boba switches (Gazzew)
- Holy Pandas, Franken-switches, most custom switch variants
- Outemu
- TTC
- Akko

**If your switch is not one of the exceptions below, it's almost certainly MX-compatible.**

---

### Alps / Matias — Different Universe

Alps switches were made by Alps Electric (Japan) and used in many beloved keyboards from the 1980s and 1990s, including Apple Extended Keyboards and many vintage Zenith and DEC boards. Modern Matias switches are spiritual successors.

The Alps stem is a rectangular post — completely different from the MX cross. **MX keycaps will not fit Alps switches, and Alps keycaps will not fit MX switches.**

Vintage Alps keycaps are sought after by collectors. Matias makes modern caps for Matias switches. These are entirely separate ecosystems.

**You will only encounter this if:** You're building with a vintage Alps switch or a Matias switch. Not relevant to most modern builds.

---

### Topre — Proprietary Capacitive System

Topre switches use a capacitive sensing mechanism with a different physical stem design. They're used exclusively in HHKB (Happy Hacking Keyboard) and Realforce boards.

Topre keycaps have a proprietary socket that does not accept MX stems. However:
- **Topre-to-MX adapters exist** (like Novatouch sliders or Hasu adapters) that fit inside Topre housings and accept MX-stemmed keycaps
- This is a common mod for HHKB users who want access to the much larger MX keycap market

Topre-native keycap selection is limited but growing. This is a niche within a niche.

---

### Low-Profile Switches — Completely Different

Low-profile keyboards use shorter switches, typically 3.5mm travel instead of 4mm. The two dominant low-profile switch systems are:

**Kailh Choc (Low Profile)**
The most common low-profile switch system. Kailh Choc switches have a completely different stem — a flat oval design that looks nothing like an MX stem. **Standard MX keycaps do not fit Choc switches at all.**

Choc switches require Choc-specific keycaps. The keycap market for Choc is significantly smaller than MX, but growing:
- MBK keycaps (the most popular Choc keycap)
- Choc PBT blank keycaps from various vendors
- Some specialty sets designed for Choc-based boards

**For Corne Low-Profile (Corne LP):**
If you're building or purchasing a Corne LP with Choc switches, you *must* use Choc-compatible keycaps. Your current Corne (with nice!nano v2) may use either MX or Choc switches depending on which PCB version you have. Check which switch type is installed before buying keycaps.

**TL;DR on Compatibility:**
```
MX switches → MX keycaps (99% of the market, vast selection)
Choc switches → Choc keycaps (smaller market but growing, MBK is the go-to)
Alps/Matias → Alps/Matias keycaps (niche/vintage)
Topre → Topre keycaps (or Topre with MX adapter)
```

---

### Row / Key Size Compatibility

Beyond stem type, keycap sets must include the right sizes for your specific layout. This matters enormously for 40% and split keyboards:

- **1u**: A single standard unit of key width (approximately 18mm x 18mm top). Home row alphas are 1u.
- **1.25u**: Slightly wider. Standard modifier size for keys like Tab, Backspace in some rows.
- **1.5u**: Tab key, backspace on some layouts.
- **2u**: A key twice as wide — some backspace keys, some enter keys.
- **2.25u**: Standard Enter key width on ANSI layouts, or Left Shift.
- **6.25u**: Standard spacebar size.
- **Various other sizes** for bottom rows, Enter variants, etc.

**For Corne:**
The Corne keyboard uses 42 keys, all 1u — there are no wide modifier keys. Every single key is the same width. This is good news: you need a set that has at least 42 1u keys in compatible colors, plus you want them to be labeled correctly for your layers if you use legends at all. Many Corne users go **blank** precisely because legends are meaningless when you have 7 layers.

## 7. RGB and Shine-Through

RGB is a massive part of keyboard culture. Keycaps interact with RGB in ways that aren't obvious.

---

### How RGB Works in Switches

Most RGB-capable switches have an LED position underneath or inside the switch housing. The light shines upward through the switch and into the keycap. Whether you actually *see* that light depends almost entirely on the keycap.

---

### Keycap Opacity and RGB

**Opaque keycaps (most ABS, most PBT):**
Light hits the bottom of the keycap and doesn't pass through. RGB is blocked entirely. You'll see diffused light around the edges of the key (through the gap between keycap and plate), which can still look good, but the light doesn't shine through the key itself.

**Semi-translucent keycaps:**
Some keycap materials, particularly thinner ABS, allow some light bleed. This is unintentional and usually looks washed-out.

**PC (Polycarbonate) shine-through keycaps:**
Fully transparent or frosted. Light passes through the keycap body completely. Maximum RGB visibility. The legend appears either as:
- A solid legend against a glowing background
- A glowing legend against a background (reverse)

**Double-shot with clear top:**
ABS double-shot where the outer shell is transparent PC/ABS and the inner legend piece is opaque. This is the premium shine-through approach — crisp opaque legends floating in a glowing transparent keycap. Used by many high-end RGB keyboard manufacturers.

---

### North-Facing vs. South-Facing Switch Orientation

This is a subtlety that catches many builders off guard, particularly with Cherry-profile keycaps.

**Switch orientation** refers to which way the LED hole in the switch faces:
- **South-facing**: LED faces toward you (toward the bottom of the board). Most common in modern keyboards.
- **North-facing**: LED faces away from you (toward the top of the board). Common in gaming keyboards marketed for RGB visibility.

**Why it matters:**

Cherry profile keycaps have a specific geometry: the top of the keycap (toward the number row) has a ledge that extends toward the switch below it. When switches are **north-facing**, this ledge on the keycap in the row *above* interferes with the bottom of the keycap in the current row. This causes a phenomenon called **north-facing interference** — the keycap wobbles, doesn't press smoothly, or bottoms out with a rattly feeling.

Additionally, south-facing switches on RGB keyboards place the LED further from the keycap legend, giving better light distribution. North-facing puts the LED directly under the top of the keycap, which can look great for shine-through but creates interference with Cherry profile.

**Practical advice:**
- If you have north-facing switches, use OEM, SA, DSA, or XDA profile to avoid interference
- Cherry profile is safest with south-facing switches
- For most Corne builds (which don't prioritize underglow shine-through), this is mostly academic — but if you're planning a build where RGB matters, check your switch orientation before buying Cherry profile caps

## 8. Keycap Sets and Buying

The keycap market has a unique culture built around scarcity, community design, and long wait times. Understanding how it works saves money and heartbreak.

---

### Group Buys

Group Buys (GBs) are the defining purchasing model of the enthusiast keyboard hobby. A designer creates a keycap set (working with a manufacturer like GMK, SP, or Drop), opens an "interest check" to gauge demand, then launches a pre-order that runs for 1–4 weeks. If minimum order quantities are met, the buy proceeds.

**Timeline:** From the end of the group buy to delivery is typically **6 to 18 months**, sometimes longer. Some GBs have taken 2+ years due to manufacturing delays, shipping issues, or production problems.

**Advantages:**
- Access to unique, often beautiful community-designed sets not available any other way
- Often high quality (GMK GBs are the benchmark)
- Community investment — you're part of something

**Disadvantages:**
- Long wait times
- If the GB fails to meet MOQ, it's cancelled (refunds issued, but disappointing)
- Risk: manufacturer delays, quality issues, company closures
- You pay upfront and wait — that's capital tied up for 1-2 years

**How to find GBs:**
- r/mechmarket (Reddit) — the hub for GB announcements and sales
- Geekhack forums — where many GBs are officially run
- Designer Instagram accounts
- Drop, NovelKeys, Cannonkeys websites all announce their own GBs

---

### In-Stock

In-stock keycaps are available immediately, no wait, buy and ship today. The selection is smaller than what's been produced through GBs, but there are genuinely excellent in-stock options.

**Best in-stock sources:**
- **Drop** (drop.com): Keeps popular sets in rotation, including some MT3 and Cherry profile sets
- **NovelKeys**: Good selection of enthusiast-grade in-stock
- **Cannonkeys**: Quality in-stock options
- **KBDfans** (kbdfans.com): Excellent Chinese manufacturer with many in-stock options, particularly PBT sets
- **Bolsa Supply**: Specialty retailer with curated selection
- **TheKeyBlog**: Australian-based, ships globally, good selection
- **Walletburner / Vendor-direct**: Some manufacturers sell direct

---

### Aftermarket / Secondhand

After a GB closes, the only way to get that set is secondhand. The keyboard hobby has a robust resale market:

- **r/mechmarket**: The primary marketplace. Listings with photos, prices in USD (plus shipping)
- **Geekhack classifieds**: Forum-based classifieds
- **Keycap Instagram communities**: DMs for trades and sales
- **Discord servers**: Many hobby servers have market channels

Popular sets on the aftermarket often go for 1.5–3x the original GB price. Some rare or discontinued sets fetch dramatically more.

---

### Clones / Replicas

The ethical debate of the keyboard hobby. Clone keycaps are copies of existing designs, usually produced by Chinese manufacturers at a fraction of the price of originals.

**Common clone sources:**
- Various AliExpress sellers
- Some KBDfans products
- Specific "GMK-clone" sets that copy popular colorways

**What you get:**
- The colorway at 20–30% of the price
- Lower quality legends (legends may be slightly blurry, less sharp)
- Less precise tolerances
- Different materials (often thinner ABS)
- No MOQ delays — often in stock

**The controversy:**
Original keycap designers and manufacturers argue clones steal the revenue that funds design work and innovation. The counterpoint is that original sets are often priced out of reach for most people globally, and clone buyers weren't going to pay full price anyway.

**Practical stance:** Clones are functional. They look reasonable from a distance. They're a valid entry point. If you buy clones and fall in love with a colorway, consider buying the original if it comes back in stock. Don't buy clones of someone's original work and publicly flaunt it in communities where the designer is present — that's considered disrespectful.

## 9. Special Keycaps for 40% and Corne

Running a 40% or split keyboard like the Corne creates specific keycap challenges and opportunities that don't apply to standard full-size or TKL boards.

---

### The 1u Problem

Standard keycap sets are designed for full-size keyboards. They typically include:
- Exactly the right number of alphanumeric keys (26 letter keys, 10 number keys)
- A small number of 1u modifiers
- Many larger modifier keys (Tab, Caps Lock, Enter, Shift, Backspace) in non-1u sizes

**The problem for Corne:**
The Corne uses 42 keys, all 1u. You don't need any wide modifier keys. But you need more 1u keys than a standard set provides for the modifier positions — and you need the right legends (or blanks) for those positions.

Many premium keycap sets don't include enough 1u modifier keys for a 40% layout. Before buying any set, verify:
1. Does it include at least 42 1u keys in total?
2. Do those keys cover the legends you want for your layout?
3. Does it include a "40% kit" or "ergodox kit" or "ortholinear kit" as an add-on?

Many GB sets sell "spacebars kit," "40% kit," and "numpad kit" as separate add-ons precisely because the base kit doesn't cover non-standard layouts.

---

### Why Blank Keycaps Are Popular on 40% / Corne

When you have 7 layers (BASE, NAV, NUM, MEDIA, SYM, FUN, MOUSE), the legend on the physical key tells you the BASE layer function only. It tells you nothing about what the key does in NAV layer or NUM layer. After a few weeks of using the keyboard, the legends become meaningless — you know the layout by muscle memory, not by reading keys.

This is why many Corne and ergo users go **completely blank**:
- No meaningless legends to distract or mislead
- Works with any layout change without looking "wrong"
- Cleaner aesthetic
- Cheaper — blank PBT is some of the most affordable quality keycap available

**Recommended blanks for Corne:**

| Set | Material | Profile | Price | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| KBDfans PBT Blank | PBT | Various | ~$15-25 | Excellent budget option |
| SP DSA Blank | ABS | DSA | ~$30-50 | Classic, many colors, from Signature Plastics |
| MBK Blank | PBT | Choc-compatible | ~$25 | **Only for Choc switch versions** |
| EnjoyPBT Blank | PBT | Various | ~$20-35 | Good quality, multiple colors |
| YMDK/AKKO Blank | PBT | Various | ~$15-25 | Budget, functional |

---

### Uniform vs. Sculpted for Corne

This bears repeating in the context of Corne specifically:

**Sculpted profiles (Cherry, OEM, SA, MT3):** Row sculpt means keys designed for Row 3 (home row) placed in a Row 1 position will have the wrong angle. On a Corne with Miryoku layers, you're constantly remapping what a physical key does. In Layer 1 (NAV), a key that's physically in row 2 might be functioning as something you'd normally find in row 4. The row sculpt creates a mild ergonomic mismatch that adds up over a long typing session.

**Uniform profiles (DSA, XDA, KAT):** Every key is identical. A key moved from any position to any other position feels exactly the same. This is the ergonomic freedom that Corne and Miryoku demand.

**Recommendation for this Corne build:** DSA or XDA, either blank or with a modest legend set.

---

### Notable Sets That Work Well on Corne

**DSA Milkshake** (Candy-inspired pastel colors)
- Profile: DSA (uniform)
- Material: PBT
- Why it works: Pastel colorways, playful aesthetic, proven Corne compatibility, enough 1u keys, available in aftermarket
- Where to find: r/mechmarket, aftermarket sales

**XDA Oblique**
- Profile: XDA (uniform)
- Material: PBT dye-sub
- Why it works: Designed with ergo/ortho users in mind, plenty of 1u coverage
- Where to find: In-stock at various vendors when available

**Any Blank PBT Set (DSA or XDA)**
- The most practical recommendation — buy a neutral color (white, gray, black, or a color that matches your aesthetic) in DSA or XDA profile, blank PBT, and enjoy the freedom of a completely label-free keyboard that you can re-learn to any layout

**KAT Milkshake / KAT Refined**
- Profile: KAT (uniform)
- Material: ABS double-shot
- Why it works: Beautiful sets with uniform profile and good 40% coverage

---

### Homing Keys on the Corne

Standard keyboards have homing bumps on F and J (the index finger home row positions) to help you find home row by touch. On the Corne, your home row mods are on A, S, D, F (left) and J, K, L, ; (right).

Many keycap sets include homing keys (with a small bump or dish) for F and J. On a blank set, you can:
1. Use the stock homing keys (F and J) on the physical F and J positions — the Corne home row aligns with standard QWERTY positions
2. Buy a separate homing kit (some vendors sell individual homing key add-ons)
3. Use a notched or raised homing key from a mixed set

This is a minor quality-of-life detail but worth considering.

## 10. Care and Maintenance

Keycaps require minimal care but benefit from occasional attention. Here's how to keep them in good condition for years.

---

### Removing Keycaps

Always use a **keycap puller**. Using your fingers risks damaging switches and can crack keycap stems.

**Wire keycap puller**: The best type. A thin wire hook that goes under two sides of the keycap and lifts evenly. Minimal risk of scratching.

**Plastic ring keycap puller**: The type often included with keyboards. Works but has more risk of scratching keycap sides. Acceptable for occasional use.

**Technique:**
1. Place the puller hooks under opposite sides of the keycap
2. Squeeze lightly to ensure the puller is seated
3. Pull straight up — no rocking, no twisting
4. If resistance is felt, don't force it; re-seat the puller

---

### Cleaning Keycaps

**Routine (every few months):**
1. Remove all keycaps (either photograph your layout first, or if you have the layout memorized or use blank keys, no need)
2. Fill a bowl with warm water and a small amount of mild dish soap
3. Drop keycaps in and let soak for 20–30 minutes
4. Gently agitate or use a soft brush (old toothbrush works) to scrub the surface and legend areas
5. Rinse thoroughly with clean water
6. Spread keycaps on a towel in a single layer and allow to **completely dry** before reinstalling — even small amounts of water trapped in the stem socket can cause corrosion on switch contacts over time. Allow 24–48 hours of air drying.

**Deep cleaning:** Same process but add a small amount of isopropyl alcohol (70%) to the water, or do a separate IPA wipe after the soap wash. IPA cuts through body oils and grease that soap leaves behind.

**Ultrasonic cleaner:** Enthusiasts often use ultrasonic jewelry cleaners with water and a small amount of dish soap. Very effective for getting into crevices and under legend indentations. Gentle on keycaps.

**Do NOT:** Use acetone, harsh solvents, or abrasive cleaners. These will damage ABS and can affect PBT coloring.

---

### Dealing with ABS Shine

ABS shine is caused by your skin's oils gradually polishing the microscopic texture of the ABS surface. There is no way to permanently reverse this on stock ABS keycaps, but you can reduce it:

**Temporary solutions:**
- Clean with IPA — removes oils and temporarily refreshes texture
- Lightly sand the keycap surface with very fine sandpaper (1000–2000 grit) — creates a new texture. This removes shine but risks affecting legends unless you're very careful.
- Toothpaste (white, not gel) applied with a soft cloth — mild abrasive that can restore some texture temporarily

**Permanent solution:**
Switch to PBT keycaps, which resist shine inherently.

**Embracing shine:**
Many people develop affection for well-used ABS keycaps. The shine pattern shows exactly which keys you use most, creating a unique "patina" that tells the story of the keyboard's use. This is especially meaningful on vintage boards. Consider it a feature.

---

### Storage

If you're rotating keycap sets (many enthusiasts own multiple sets they swap seasonally or per-project):
- Store in a clean, dry container
- The original keycap tray from the set is ideal — designed to hold the keycaps safely
- Keep away from direct sunlight (UV causes yellowing in white ABS and some light-colored PBT over years)
- Keep away from high humidity
- A zip-lock bag works in a pinch; silica gel packs help control moisture

## 12. Artisan Keycaps

Artisan keycaps are one of the most fascinating subcultures within the already niche keyboard hobby. They are individually handcrafted keycaps — usually for a single key (most often the Escape key) — that feature detailed sculptural work, often depicting characters, creatures, logos, or abstract art.

---

### What Is an Artisan Keycap?

An artisan keycap replaces a functional keycap with a miniature work of art. They are:
- **Handmade** in small batches (sometimes single pieces) by individual makers (called "artisans")
- **Cast in resin** using silicone molds, with hand-painted details, glitter, inclusions, or other effects
- **Priced as art** — from $20 for entry-level makers to $500+ for highly sought-after pieces from established artisans
- **Collectible** — the artisan keycap market has a collector economy rivaling sneakers or trading cards

---

### Materials and Process

Most artisan keycaps are cast in **UV resin** or **epoxy resin**:

1. The artisan sculpts a master design in clay or polymer clay
2. A silicone mold is made from the master
3. Resin (often UV-curable) is poured into the mold with pigments, glitter, glow-in-the-dark powder, mica powder, or other inclusions
4. UV light or time cures the resin
5. The finished cap is removed, trimmed, and often hand-painted with fine detail
6. An MX-compatible stem is embedded or the stem socket is part of the mold

The result is a keycap with sculptural depth impossible to achieve with standard injection molding.

---

### Notable Artisan Types and Styles

**Sculpted characters**: Gaming characters, anime characters, fantasy creatures, original characters. A tiny skull, a cat, a wizard's hat, a dragon coiled around the key.

**Nature-inspired**: Mushrooms, flowers, coral, crystal formations. These translate particularly well to resin.

**Glow-in-the-dark (GITD)**: Phosphorescent pigments that charge in light and glow in the dark. Popular for gaming builds.

**Color-shifting / Aurora**: Special pigments that shift color under different lighting angles. Extremely popular.

**Custom portrait work**: Some artisans sculpt portraits of beloved pets, characters, or people to commission.

**"Jelly" series**: Semi-translucent resin keycaps in candy-like colors. Very popular with RGB builds since light shines through.

---

### Established Artisans (Historical / Ongoing)

The artisan community is extremely active. Some respected names (note: availability varies; this hobby moves fast):

- **Jelly Key** (Vietnam) — produces extremely detailed nature-inspired resin keycaps at scale. More accessible pricing ($20-80). Very popular starter artisan.
- **Dwarf Factory** — known for highly detailed character sculpts
- **Schmidtt Studio** — beloved for candy-themed and pastel aesthetics
- **SkluzePOD** — collectible, highly sought-after
- **Clack Factory** — legendary in the community, very limited releases, extremely high aftermarket value
- **Brocaps** — high quality sculpted keycaps
- Various one-person makers on Etsy, Instagram, and Discord

---

### How the Artisan Market Works

The artisan market has its own ecosystem:

**Raffles (Raffles / "Raff"):**
Because artisans make small quantities, selling directly creates chaos (site crashes, bots buying everything in milliseconds). The solution: raffles. You enter your name/email, winner is selected randomly, winner has 24–48 hours to pay. Many popular artisans run all their sales via raffle.

**Hive (Keycap Hive / Hive):**
A dedicated platform (keycaplendar.info / keycaphive) that aggregates artisan sales, raffles, and announcements. The hub for tracking what's available when.

**Aftermarket:**
Popular artisan keycaps from desirable makers resell on r/mechmarket for 2–10x their original price. A Clack Factory keycap that raffled at $40 might sell secondhand for $300+.

**Commission:**
Some artisans accept direct commissions for custom designs. Expect higher prices (often $50–200+) and longer wait times.

---

### Fit and Compatibility

Most artisan keycaps are designed to be MX-compatible. However:

- Because they're handcast, tolerances can be slightly looser or tighter than injection-molded keycaps
- Some artisans are known for tight fits; some for loose. Community reviews on r/mechmarket, Discord, and forums will mention this.
- Most artisans target the Escape key position (1u top-left). This is the "display" key of the keyboard — visible, prominent, rarely used in the middle of typing.
- Some artisans also make spacebar and modifier artisans, but 1u Escape is the dominant format.

---

### Artisans on a Corne

The Corne doesn't have a traditional Escape key in the top-left position — the top-left physical key is typically Q or some modifier depending on your layer setup. Your current layout has `ESC` accessible through the MEDIA layer (holding the left inner thumb key). 

However, artisan keycaps work on any 1u key. On a Corne, people typically place an artisan on a key that is:
1. Physically prominent and visible (top-left alpha row corner is natural)
2. Not critical during heavy typing (a key that's held as a layer access key while you're looking at it)
3. Aesthetically anchoring — the artisan becomes the focal point of the build

Many Corne builders place artisans on the thumb cluster keys, which are very visible and often feel like the "signature" of a split ergo build.

---

### Caring for Artisan Keycaps

- **Don't soak in water** — resin can fog or develop micro-cracks with prolonged water exposure
- **Keep out of direct sunlight** — UV light yellows some resins over time
- **Don't clean with IPA or harsh solvents** — can dull the resin surface finish
- **Clean gently** with a slightly damp cloth
- **Store carefully** — many artisans come with custom boxes or pouches; use them

## Summary and Recommendations for Your Corne Build

Given your setup (Corne with nice!nano v2, ZMK firmware, 7-layer Miryoku-style layout, 42 keys), here's a practical purchase roadmap:

### Immediate Upgrade Path

**If you want to understand profiles first** → Start with DSA or XDA blank PBT from KBDfans ($20-35). Get a feel for uniform profiles on a Corne before spending more.

**If you want to look good and feel great immediately** → DSA Milkshake on aftermarket, or any in-stock XDA set with enough 1u keys, or blank PBT in your favorite color.

**If you want to go deep on feel** → Save up for MT3 or GMK (understanding you'll need uniform profile or accept the sculpt mismatch), or wait for a KAT or XDA group buy with an ergo kit.

**If you love RGB** → Look for double-shot ABS with clear/PC top layer, or translucent PC caps, ensuring they're in a uniform profile.

**If you want to be a collector** → Enter the artisan raffle scene. Start with Jelly Key (accessible, beautiful, available) and work up from there.

---

### The Hierarchy of Decisions

1. **Stem compatibility**: MX or Choc? Don't skip this step.
2. **Profile**: Uniform (DSA/XDA) for Corne — this is the most important decision.
3. **Material**: PBT for durability and texture; ABS for color vibrancy.
4. **Legend method**: Double-shot > dye-sub > everything else.
5. **Aesthetics**: The fun part — color, design, artisans.
6. **Price**: Spend what the hobby brings you joy at, not what the community says you "should" spend.

The keyboard hobby is genuinely deep — people have been optimizing this since the 1970s. Keycaps are the most personal, most visible, and most iteratively explored dimension of any build. Enjoy the exploration.
